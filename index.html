about

A mechanical engineer who writes the models

I'm a Computer Science PhD candidate in the Vision & AI Lab (VAIL) at the University of Texas at San Antonio, advised by Dr. Amanda Fernandez. My research is multimodal machine learning for industrial inspection and anomaly detection, with a focus on laser powder bed fusion (LPBF).

I came to ML from the shop floor. My undergraduate degree is in mechanical engineering, and I spent three years training more than 100 students on lathes, mills and laser cutters. At Idaho National Laboratory I designed hardware for reactor experiments, and at Pacific Northwest National Laboratory I tested whether explainable AI recovers known chemistry from a nuclear reprocessing simulator. I treat ML as a tool in service of physical systems, and I care whether a model's answer survives contact with the hardware.

I currently hold a Graduate Research Organization (GRO) appointment at Oak Ridge National Laboratory, working with Luke Scime on fast processing of near- and long-wave infrared video from powder bed fusion builds.

  • Learning from very small, expensive datasets, where every printed coupon counts.
  • Fusing modalities (images, sensor traces, process parameters, text) and testing when fusion actually helps.
  • Algorithms fast enough to keep up with a live build instead of a hard drive full of video.
  • Keeping the physics in view: heat transfer, porosity mechanisms, and what the sensor can really see.

Featured · side project · PyTorch

Rebuilding a temperature field from six sensors, with physics as the teacher

A physics-informed neural network that reconstructs a transient temperature field from sparse point sensors and noisy low-resolution camera frames. The heat equation and convective boundary conditions are part of the loss. I checked every result against finite-difference solvers I wrote myself, first on a 2D plate and then on a 3D finned aluminum heat sink.

FINDING 01

Six sensors are enough when the physics is right

With only six point sensors plus the heat equation, the network reconstructs the whole plate. Ten minutes into heating, its error is within about 0.3% of the field's range. The physics fills in everything the sensors can't see.

FINDING 02

Adding a second modality made it worse

I trained the same network three ways: sensors only, camera only, and both fused. Fusion helped early in the transient (0.39 K vs 0.53 K at 88 s). Later on, the noisy camera pulled the fit toward its own noise, and sensors alone came out ahead (0.25 K vs 0.53 K at 600 s).

A second modality only pays off when it fills a gap the stronger one can't see. That question drives my PhD work too.

FINDING 03

The loss curve said it worked. It hadn't.

On the heat sink, physics-only training converged with clean loss curves, yet the fins never warmed above 0 K. The heat source is a 1.5 mm spot in a 28 mm domain split across 13 fins. Random collocation points almost never landed on it, so a cold field satisfied the equation nearly everywhere the network checked.

Concentrating samples at the source and adding six virtual sensors cut max error from ~11 K to ~4 K on a field that peaks near 13 K. That's still imperfect, and the figure shows where: the outer fins run warm.

PNNL · 2024–2025

Does explainable AI find the chemistry? Testing it on a reprocessing simulator

The goal was to trace where a PUREX solvent-extraction simulator disagrees with experiments. But before you trust an explainable-AI method on real discrepancies, you have to know it finds physics, not noise. My internship built that pipeline and tested it on simulated discrepancies, where chemistry already tells you the answer.

I worked from 150 existing simulator runs varying three inputs: aqueous feed flow, organic feed flow and TBP solvent concentration. I aligned every run to a common duration and sampling rate, computed its error against the run closest to the Hanford PUREX flowsheet, modeled that error with random forests and LSTMs, and attributed it with SHAP.

It passed. SHAP ranked the inputs the way extraction chemistry predicts, and for feed flow it got the direction right too: more uranium coming in pushed the error up. The next step was pointing it at experimental data.

Recovered the expected drivers: TBP and feed flow ahead of organic flow

INL · 2022–2023

Retractable in-core sensor mechanism

Sensors in reactor experiments drift out of calibration under long exposure. The fix I worked on: a thermocouple that a drive pushes through 20 ft of 1/8" guide tubing into the test region for a reference reading, then pulls back out.

I built the drive from the feed rolls of a MIG welding gun and a geared 12 V motor, joined by custom 3D-printed brackets on 1" extrusion. A carriage rides a rail between two limit switches that end each stroke, and a control box I wired handles insert, retract and e-stop.

Drive force ≈ 20 N (peak 33 N) · needed through offset tubing ≈ 3 N ~40% smaller system footprint

UTSA × SwRI · senior design · 2022

Propellant management device for a micro-gravity test bench

In orbit there's no gravity to hold liquid oxygen over a tank's outlet, so spacecraft rely on surface tension: vanes and sponges that wick propellant to the drain. Southwest Research Institute built a neutral-buoyancy test bench to study these devices on the ground, and my senior design project was a device for it.

As design engineer I worked on the geometry and the assembly drawings: a perforated core ringed by 28 sponge fins, with 4 curved vanes that follow the wall of a 15-inch spherical tank. Fins and vanes slot in and swap out without rebuilding the core, so new configurations can be tested cheaply. It's built from clear polycarbonate, so you can watch the fluid, and stainless steel.

Capillary analysis: no dripping at 0.001–0.002 g · checked in Surface Evolver and FEA

Side project · FLIR Boson LWIR

Thermal inspection of a running steam machine

I pulled the front panel off a steam machine while it was running and filmed it with a longwave infrared camera. The honeycomb heater grid is the hottest thing in view, and from the side the heat spreads across the top of the casing.

Does the grid heat evenly? I compared every patch of honeycomb with the rest of its own panel across 64 frames. No interior patch ran consistently cooler. The only persistent cold readings sat on the frame rails and the far edge of the grid, where the camera sees the cells at an angle.

The app recorded color video with auto-contrast on, so I recovered a relative scale from the color palette and only compared spots within the same frame. Lens specks are filled with real pixels from aligned neighboring frames, not painted over.
